Sixteenth Finance Commission
Annexure 11.1
Methodology of Disaster Risk Index
The Disaster Risk Index (DRI) is a function of Hazard, Exposure and Vulnerability. Hazard refers to the
possible future occurrence of natural or human-induced physical events that may have adverse effects
on vulnerable and exposed elements. Exposure refers to the inventory of elements in an area in which
hazard events may occur. Vulnerability refers to the propensity of exposed elements such as human
beings, their livelihoods, and assets to suffer adverse effects when impacted by hazard events. FC-16’s
methodology to calculate DRI considers all three pillars – Hazard, Exposure and Vulnerability.
Hazards
A comprehensive and contextually relevant approach to hazard identification has been adopted, covering
a wide range of hydro-meteorological and geological hazards prevalent in India. Specifically, the
Commission considered cyclones, floods, droughts, landslides, earthquakes, cloudbursts, hailstorms and
cold waves – eight of the twelve disasters notified at the national level by the Union Government. The
availability of reliable, State-disaggregated data from official sources and national repositories informed
the selection of these hazards.
In addition to the above eight disasters, heat waves and lightning have been included as part of our
assessment in the State-specific disaster category. While these events are currently classified as State-
specific hazards due to their localised nature, their increasing frequency, severity and geographic spread
across multiple States indicate the need to consider them for future inclusion in the list of nationally
notified disasters.
A structured and objective scoring framework has been adopted to estimate hazard scores for each State.
The raw hazard values were classified into five ordinal risk classes and assigned scores of 3, 6, 9, 12 and
15, respectively. These scores reflect increasing levels of hazard intensity across States.
For the classification of hazard data into these ordinal risk classes, the quintile classification was used
and calculated the breaks at 20, 40, 60 and 80 percentiles, respectively. This approach ensures that each
class contains approximately an equal number of States. This approach is useful for comparative ranking
across a fixed number of entities (such as States) and its ability to effectively distinguish the relative risk
profile of States.
The score of each hazard is then summed after applying weightages based on disaster wise expenditure
from the NDMIS – Flood (50 per cent), Drought (15 per cent), Cyclone (7 per cent), Landslide (5 per
cent), State-Specific Disasters (15 per cent), Earthquake (2 per cent), hailstorm (2 per cent), Cold wave
(2 per cent) and Cloud Burst (2 per cent). While the share of the reported expenditure for certain disasters
is less, the weight applied to the latter four disasters and to landslides has been increased to 2 per cent
each and 5 per cent, respectively, to give them their fair share in the hazard calculation.
Flood
Floods in India arise primarily from three distinct sources: (i) riverine flooding, which largely affects the
extensive floodplains of northern and eastern India; (ii) intense rainfall during cyclones, which impacts
coastal States and adjoining inland regions; and (iii) flash floods, often triggered by heavy and very
heavy rainfall events, particularly in hilly and urbanised areas.
